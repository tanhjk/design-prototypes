Part 01

The story

The block was in Jalan Kukoh and the lift was out, so Vera walked up nine floors with a clipboard, a bag of rice and almost no idea what she was going to say. She was seventeen, a week into a school volunteering programme, and the door she knocked on was opened by a woman who had been asked the same questions by four different organisations that year.

“She was very polite about it,” Vera says. “She told me, you all keep coming to ask, and nothing changes. I did not have an answer. I have been trying to build one ever since.”

The rental blocks are where her interest in policy started, but they are not where she expected it to lead. She had assumed she would become a social worker — the person at the door. A teacher pointed out that the forms the social worker was filling in had been designed by someone else, in an office, years earlier, and that those forms decided as much as the visit did.

She applied for the PSC Scholarship (Public Administration) in her final year of junior college, and spent the interview talking about the nine floors.

“I did not have a grand thesis about inequality,” she says. “I had one household, one conversation, and a very specific thing that had gone wrong. I think that is what they wanted to hear. The panel kept pulling me back to the details.”

London was harder than the scholarship brochures suggest, in ways that had nothing to do with grades. She arrived at the London School of Economics to read social policy and economics, and found herself in seminars where Singapore came up mainly as a case study — usually flattened into a single paragraph about public housing, usually by someone who had never been.

“I got very good at saying ‘it is more complicated than that’,” she says. “Then I realised I was doing the same thing to Britain. That was a useful year.”

Policy is not the opposite of being on the ground. It is what happens to the ground when you are not looking.

She came back in 2023 and was posted to the Ministry of Social and Family Development, which is where the nine floors caught up with her. One of her first assignments was a review of how often families on assistance had to re-declare the same information to different agencies.

The part she wants prospective applicants to hear: a public administration scholarship is not a fast track to influence. “Your first two years are spreadsheets, minutes and reading other people’s drafts,” she says. “If you need the work to feel important every day, this will be a long six years.”

If you are applying this cycle

Panels for the administrative awards tend to probe one example hard rather than range across many. Pick something you were actually in the room for — including the parts that did not work — and be ready to be asked what you would do differently.

Part 02

The work

Vera works on what the ministry calls “the application journey” — the sequence of forms, checks and appointments a household passes through between asking for help and receiving it. Her current file is a narrow one, and has taken two years: whether three separate schemes can accept a single declaration of income.

A normal week is less cinematic than the recruitment videos suggest.

  • Two days of data. Anonymised case records, mostly in Excel, mostly finding out that the pattern you were sure about is an artefact of how a field was coded in 2019.
  • One day of meetings. With the agencies who would have to change their forms, none of whom asked for this.
  • One day of writing. Submissions, ministerial replies, and a paper that has been through eleven drafts.
  • One day on the ground. Family service centres and social service offices, sitting in on intake sessions when families agree to it.

What the bond actually feels like

Vera is three years into six. She is candid that the bond shapes decisions in ways that are easy to underestimate at eighteen and impossible to ignore at twenty-seven.

“A friend moved to a think tank in Geneva last year and I read the job posting twice before closing it,” she says. “That is what a bond is. It is not a prison, it is a door you walk past.”

The trade she says she would make again: a seat in the room where the forms are designed, at twenty-four, with no loan. “The people I volunteered with are still knocking on doors, and they are better at it than I ever was,” she says. “My job is to make sure fewer of those visits are needed. Ask me again in year six.”

Part 03

Ask me anything #AMA

We put reader questions to Vera. These are her answers, lightly edited for length.

Do you actually get to change anything in your first posting?

Not on your own, and not quickly. What you get early is the ability to put a question on the table that nobody had asked. My first accepted recommendation was one sentence long and took fourteen months. That is a normal ratio.

Is a social policy degree worth it, or should I read economics?

The ministry is full of economists and it is not short of them. What was scarce in my first year was people who could read a case file and a regression with the same attention. If you can only pick one, pick the one you will argue with rather than absorb.

What did your interview panel actually want to hear?

Specifics. I talked about one household in a rental block and a form that asked them the same question three times, and why that happened. Everyone says they care about inequality. Very few can describe one concrete thing that went wrong in front of them.

Does volunteering help an application, or does everyone do it?

Everyone does it, so the hours are worth nothing by themselves. What counted was that I had kept going back to the same block for three years and could say what had changed and what had not. Depth reads very differently from a list.

Be honest — would you pick a shorter bond if you could go back?

I would ask harder about what the bond years look like, which is a different question from how long they are. Six years on work you believe in is shorter than four years of waiting to be trusted. Nobody asked me “what will you be doing in year three” and I wish they had.
