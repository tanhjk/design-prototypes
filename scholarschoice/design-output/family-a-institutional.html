Adapted to the visual system already proven on the LTC Lou Yang sponsored feature — graphite, bone and cobalt; Playfair Display over DM Sans; DM Serif italic for anything in the subject's own voice.

Palette change — navy & gold → graphite, bone & cobalt

Gold is gone, and so is the navy. The side-by-side study showed the navy was carrying as much of the heritage read as the gold was — deep navy on cream paper is the convention; gold was the ornament on it. So the whole palette moved: graphite grounds, bone paper, and cobalt as the only chromatic colour on the page. A cool slate carries labels and the 3px card rules.

Cobalt splits by ground, because it has to. Cobalt against graphite measures 2.23:1 — as text or as a button fill on a dark panel it would have no edge at all. Gold escaped this only by being light. So: cobalt on light grounds (7.34:1 as a link, 7.94:1 as a fill under white text), and powder #9DB0FA on dark for display type, with #B8C8FC for anything under 24px. Buttons invert — cobalt fill on light, bone fill with cobalt text on dark.

Search button, per review: graphite fill with a light cobalt label — 10.73:1, Lc 72.9, and a 17.7:1 edge against the white search pod. It reads as part of the field rather than as a second CTA competing with the hero.

Separation from Family C: C is navy plus warm ember. This is graphite plus cool cobalt. No shared hue, no shared ground.

Typography change — Bebas Neue → Playfair Display

Weight ceiling honoured: the face is loaded at 400 / 500 / 600 only. Titles 2.45rem and above use 500 (Medium); everything smaller uses 600 (SemiBold), which is the hard ceiling. Nothing on the page is set heavier.

This was a retune, not a find-and-replace. Bebas is a condensed all-caps grotesque with no lowercase; Playfair is a wide, high-contrast serif with a large lowercase. Swapping the family alone would have produced oversized, loose, tightly-leaded headlines. Every display rule was re-set: sizes down 12–18%, letter-spacing from positive (+.02 to +.04em, which caps need) to negative (−.01 to −.024em, which lowercase serifs need), and line-height up from 0.92–1.05 to 1.02–1.28 to clear the descenders.

Knock-on: headlines now read in sentence case rather than forced caps, so they carry more words at the same width — the homepage hero and story decks gained roughly two words each.

LTC Lou Yang is a 37-year-old Commanding Officer of the RSAF's 123 Squadron — one of the few women to fly naval helicopters off frigates at sea. She is also an SAF Merit Scholar who read Biomedical Engineering at Duke and East Asian Studies at Yale.

Topics MINDEFRSAFOverseasEngineeringBondedUniformed servicesJC route

1 The Story

How the decision actually got made

A Singapore Youth Flying Club talk at her junior college changed everything. There was no grand plan — just a chance session, a first flight, and what she calls falling in love with flying.

The scholarship came after, not before. By the time the SAF Merit offer arrived, the question was no longer whether to serve but where the degrees would take her.

What the family said

Her parents were split, which turned out to be useful. Arguing both sides out loud made the trade-offs concrete in a way a comparison table never had.

The SAF difference: careers are planned with life stages in mind. The Servicewomen Network actively surfaces issues unique to women in uniform — and gets results.

"At work, I give my best, so that when I return to my children, I can be fully present with them."
— LTC Lou Yang, SAF Merit Scholarship recipient

2 The Work

What the job turned out to be

Deployments last a week or two, sometimes a month. At sea she manages complex multi-crew operations, coordinating navy deck crews and air force personnel in real time — landing a 20-metre Seahawk on a deck that will not stay still.

Between postings there has been plans work, flight leadership, a diplomatic aide role between air forces, and media lead on the Shangri-La Dialogue. The engineering degree earns its keep in places the brochure never mentioned.

3 What Surprised Me

Three things nobody mentions

The cohort matters more than the course. The bond gets shorter psychologically once it actually starts. And returning to SYFC as the pilot rather than the student is stranger, and better, than she expected.

The bigger picture: "We are comfortable and confident knowing what we do keeps the public and our families safe. We say we have done a great job when life carries on as usual."

Guides & Tips · Money

Read the bond before you sign it

Standfirst — own section, not an H2 Six years, four years, "service obligation" — the wording varies, and so does what it actually costs you. A plain-English walkthrough of the five clauses that matter.

By Editorial · Updated 12 January 2027 · 8 min read

SEO structure fix — the standfirst sits above and outside the heading tree. Body uses a single H1, then sequential H2 › H3. No decorative headings.

Almost every bonded scholarship in Singapore uses one of three contract shapes. Once you can tell them apart, the comparison table stops being a wall of numbers.

1. Bond length is not the same as bond start

Two awards both listed as "six years" can differ by two full years depending on when the clock starts — graduation, commissioning, or completion of initial training.

What to look for in the letter

The phrase "commencing upon" does almost all the work here. Find it before you compare anything else.

2. Coverage is a list, and lists have gaps

  • Tuition — almost always covered in full
  • Maintenance allowance — varies widely by study location
  • Airfare — often one return trip per academic year, not per term
  • Settling-in and computer grants — one-off, easy to miss

3. Breaking a bond has a formula

Liquidated damages are usually pro-rated against served time. Ask for the formula, not the headline figure.
